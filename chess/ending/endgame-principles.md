# Chess Endgame Principles

## Overview

Endgame principles are the general strategic guidelines that apply once most pieces have been exchanged and the king becomes a much more active piece than it was in the opening/middlegame. They apply to any player who already knows how pieces move and wants to convert an advantage (or hold a disadvantage) in the final phase of a game, where exact calculation often decides the result. This document does not explain how any piece moves; that is assumed prior knowledge.

## Key Concepts

### King activity

**Category:** General endgame principle.

**Why it works:** With few pieces left on the board, the king's own fighting power (not just its safety) becomes decisive — a king near the center can support its own pawns or attack the opponent's much faster than one stuck in a corner. Endgames are frequently decided by whose king reaches a key area of the board first.

### Opposition

**Category:** King-vs-king endgame technique.

**Why it works:** When two kings face each other with an odd number of squares between them on the same file, rank, or diagonal, whoever is NOT required to move next is said to "have the opposition" — this matters because the side forced to move must give way, letting the opposing king advance or gain access to a key square it needs (e.g. to escort a pawn to promotion). Opposition is a tool for forcing the other king to yield ground, not a goal in itself.

### Zugzwang

**Category:** General endgame/tactical concept (most decisive in endgames because there are few legal moves available).

**Why it works:** Zugzwang describes a position where a side would be fine if it were not forced to move, but every available move actively worsens its position. Endgames frequently reduce to very few legal moves, so creating a zugzwang (forcing the opponent to move when no safe move exists) is a common way to win a position that would otherwise be a draw.

### The rule of the square

**Category:** Pawn-race calculation shortcut (king vs. passed pawn).

**Why it works:** To quickly judge whether a king can catch a pawn racing to promote without counting out every move, draw an imaginary square from the pawn to its promotion square, with the pawn's file as one side. If the defending king can enter that square on its move, it catches the pawn; if not, the pawn promotes unopposed. This shortcut avoids calculating the full move sequence in simple king-and-pawn races.

### Triangulation

**Category:** King-maneuvering technique (closely related to opposition).

**Why it works:** Triangulation is a maneuver where one king moves along the three sides of a triangle of squares to lose a tempo deliberately — arriving at the same square as before, but with the opponent now forced to move (i.e., having passed the obligation to move onto them). It is used specifically to hand the opposition to the opponent when direct approach would hand it away instead.

## Comparison with Related Concepts

- **Opposition vs. triangulation**: Opposition describes a static relationship between the two kings' positions; triangulation is an active maneuver used to obtain that relationship when it is not already present.
- **Zugzwang vs. opposition**: Opposition is one specific, well-defined cause of zugzwang in king endings; zugzwang is the broader phenomenon and can also arise from pawn moves or other piece constraints, not only from king placement.

## Applicability

These principles apply to any player converting a small material or positional edge (or defending a slightly worse position) once the game has simplified to few pieces, most commonly king-and-pawn endgames, where precise technique rather than tactics tends to decide the result.
